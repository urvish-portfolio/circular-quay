The route here

From a factory floor to a dining room

Operations found me at university.

I led a non-technical event at an engineering college. The budget was fixed, so we negotiated, and I marketed it by visiting nearby colleges myself. It drew more participants than any event on the programme, technical ones included. On the day, one unplanned problem followed another, and keeping everything moving felt like the work I wanted to do.

The first job was a factory floor in Gujarat. Vikram Engineering Works makes spinning rings for the textile industry, and there I was responsible for 33 people, with zero accidents and orders shipped to 11 states. Broken systems and bottlenecks are what I notice first, and a production line gave that habit somewhere to work.

In January 2022 I moved to Birmingham for an MBA at Aston and joined Greggs a month later. The store had lost its manager, training had stopped and daily sales were down 25–30%. The accent and the pace were both new to me, and after one early shift I came close to quitting. I stayed. Before long I was running that store without a manager above me and covering two more sites every week.

The fixes were practical. Sandwiches were made to what actually sold, savouries were baked as the shelf ran low, ordering followed weekdays, weather and local events, and a third person joined the lunchtime peak. Sales rose 20% and waste fell 30%, with no extra resources.

Fresh Bake in Leicester was a new brand opening its first store. I helped build it from the layout up, then ran it alone after my manager left on 3 June 2024. Case 3 tells that story.

Shake Shack came next. I trained at Canary Wharf, where I was Manager-on-Duty on a site trading more than £90,000 a week, then opened Birmingham in December 2024. The brand's standards trace back to Danny Meyer's Union Square Hospitality Group, and the floor ran on them: food run to tables, orders read back, every complaint handled by a manager in person.

The next step is the one this role describes: a bigger floor, senior leaders to learn from, and a path to running a venue of my own.

    The forecast

    The day's sales forecast set first, with labour, prep and every station planned to it on the floorplan, the day's staffing map.

  2. Pre-shift briefing

  3. Doors

    The welcome

    A host at the door to greet every guest and thank them on the way out, names used and orders read back.

  4. The rush

    Off the station

    Once service is on time I step back to watch the whole room and move people to wherever the pressure is.

  5. Every 90 minutes

    The travel path

    A set walk of thirteen checkpoints from the front door to the stores, so a shortage or a slip shows up before a guest finds it.

  6. Afternoon

    The numbers

    Till sales and labour checked against the forecast, the rota adjusted, and breaks covered two at a time.

  7. Evening

    The tables

    Food run, refills offered, plates checked at the pass, where food leaves the kitchen, and any complaint taken to the guest by a manager.

    Guest feedback read, low scorers contacted, causes logged and fixed, and the next floorplan written.

Case 1 · The numbers behind the floor

Reading the count, line by line

Twice a month I wrote Birmingham's cost-of-goods report. One period the stock count came back about £4,000 below what the system expected.

A total that size would normally go straight up the line as a loss. Instead I lined the biggest movements up against the previous period. Most of the swing was the previous period's gain reversing, left behind when the stock system had been down. Across the two periods the net movement was under £40, and one product line stood out as the thing actually worth investigating.

The same habit ran through the rest of the week: a commercial report on sales against budget, labour, average spend and sales per labour hour, and a weekly fight over delivery refunds. Challenging the platform's refunds on our orders won back 70% of the value refunded between May and July 2025.

Case 2 · A service under pressure

Buy one, get one, all day

International Burger Day, 28 May 2025: every burger buy-one-get-one-free, and a queue behind the shutter by 10:30.

Staffing was ready for the crowd. What arrived at 11:00, exactly as the doors opened, was a delivery of about £4,000 of fresh and frozen stock at the back door. Everyone had ordered big for a big day, and none of it could sit outside losing temperature.

The call was to take the two slower team members off Expo, where every order is checked before it goes out, and give them 15 to 20 minutes to put it all away. That left me running Expo alone, with 15 to 20 orders on the screen and the hot side cooking as fast as it could. Because most of the team had been cross-trained, people whose stations were under control came across to help.

At 11:30 the pair came back and I moved to the dining room, where one colleague was looking after 120 to 150 seats alone. When service was back on time I came off the stations altogether: restocking from the back, temperature checks, compliance and guests. After 15:00 the team took breaks two at a time.

The day closed at about £11,000 after discounts, roughly double a normal day's trade, with no friction on the floor. The team enjoyed it as much as I did.

Case 3 · A brand's first store

The youngest at the table

Fresh Bake was a new brand, and its Leicester store was the first. Before opening I co-designed the layout with the Operations Director, worked on the sales scenarios and the snagging list, and hired the opening team: 17 people in four days.

Too many people had a say and the chain of command was unclear. I was also the youngest in the room, so most of my suggestions were tested against someone else's first. More often than not, the store came back to them in the end.

The uniform quote looked high to me. After my shifts I researched local suppliers in Leicester and kept negotiating until one met my price, then did the same for ingredients. Nobody heard about it until the saving was real; the Operations Director checked it himself before we switched.

From 3 June 2024, when my manager left, I ran the store alone. The contract said 45 hours a week. Most weeks were 65 to 70, because I wanted that store to succeed.

Case 4 · People

The conversation before the decision

At Shake Shack, one new team member was so slow that I was ready to end his probation.

Before deciding, I talked to him after a shift as a friend rather than his manager. He told me about a condition I had not known about, and then showed me how to give him work in a way that suited him. I rebuilt his tasks around his strengths. Two months later he was Employee of the Month.

Later, after one mistake in front of a senior guest, there was pressure to let him go. I asked for a month. Nobody raised it again.

At Fresh Bake, a young man new to the UK struggled through his interview, and my first instinct was no. Then I thought about my own first weeks at Greggs, when someone had given me the chance to get through. I hired him and trained him on guest experience. After I left, he became the store's Assistant Manager.

Questions employers ask

Why this role?

Restaurants won me over because a million things can go wrong in a day and no two days are the same. Putting them right gives me a real sense of relief. A large, high-volume venue at Circular Quay has more of those days than most, plus a General Manager and Assistant General Manager to learn from while I solve them. This role is the step between running a floor and running a venue, and I want to earn it.

What will I bring with me?

The parts of the job nobody sees from the dining room, done as if the venue were mine. The rest of this page covers the floor, the guest and the numbers; these sit behind them.

Rotas and floorplans
The management rota and the holiday plan for the whole site, and a floorplan for every trading day with each person placed and each break covered. On training days, trainees rotated through the stations hour by hour.
Ordering and deliveries
Weekly orders to 11 suppliers across about 180 product lines, each one placed before its cut-off. Every delivery checked against its purchase order, with shortages and credits chased.
Reporting upwards
The site P&L reviewed every period, with action taken on labour, cost of goods and controllable costs. The week's numbers walked through with my area manager, and the site presented on brand-audit walk-rounds.
Compliance paperwork
A co-developed allergen guide covering 85 products and the 14 regulated allergens, a shelf-life guide and risk-assessment sign-offs. The 48-hour exclusion rule applied after illness. Food Safety, Fire Marshal and First Aid at Level 2.
People processes
Crew learning plans and assessments, cross-training records, and employee-relations cases handled through a formal HR framework.
Getting ready for big days
Trial runs with the team before busy periods, and food sent to the neighbours before an opening to build goodwill.
An engineer's toolkit
A B.Tech in Mechanical Engineering, three and a half years in manufacturing, 5S and planograms on the shop floor, an MBA in Operations Management, and Excel and Power BI for forecasting.
